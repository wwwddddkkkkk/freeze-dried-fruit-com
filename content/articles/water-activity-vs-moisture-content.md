Freeze-dried fruit quality is often described with one word: dry. In practice, buyers need a more precise vocabulary. A product can feel dry and test low in moisture, yet still need package-and-storage validation before anyone concludes that its texture, shelf life, or safety outcome is established.

That is why two measurements matter: **moisture content** and **water activity**. They are related, but they are not the same.

## The direct answer

Moisture content measures how much water is in the product. Water activity measures how available that water is under the measurement conditions. In freeze-dried fruit, moisture content helps describe the drying result, while water activity adds information relevant to storage behavior.

For buyers, the best freeze-dried fruit quality conversations include both numbers.

## What moisture content tells you

The [process guide](/articles/how-its-made/) explains where water is removed; the two measurements below help describe the finished result rather than proving that every drying step was controlled correctly.

Moisture content is commonly reported as a percentage, but buyers should confirm the reporting basis and test method. For example, if a result is reported as 3% moisture on the stated basis, roughly 3% of that reported sample weight is water.

This number helps buyers understand the supplier's drying endpoint. It is most useful when paired with the supplier's specification, sampling plan, and acceptance range instead of being compared with a generic number from another fruit or format.

Moisture content can be considered alongside a texture check. A product that has gained moisture may feel softer, bend, clump, or lose a clean snap, but a single moisture result does not diagnose the cause or prove a storage outcome.

But moisture content has limits. It does not tell you how tightly that water is held inside the food structure.

## What water activity tells you

Water activity, often written as aw, measures how available water is inside the product. It is reported on a scale from 0 to 1. Pure water is close to 1. Very dry foods are much lower.

For freeze-dried fruit, water activity is important because it adds information about water availability beyond total water alone. It can support storage and packaging evaluation, but it does not independently predict crunch, shelf life, or food safety for every product.

This does not mean water activity is the only spec that matters. It means it gives a different kind of information than moisture content.

## Why two products with the same moisture can behave differently

Imagine two freeze-dried fruit samples that both test at 3% moisture. One stays crisp in a pouch. The other softens faster after opening.

Several product and measurement factors can help explain the difference:

- fruit variety and structure
- sugar and acid composition
- piece thickness
- drying cycle and endpoint
- packaging barrier
- desiccant strategy
- storage humidity before testing

Moisture content tells you the amount of water. Water activity helps show whether that water is more or less available.

## Why buyers should ask for both

If you are sourcing freeze-dried fruit, ask suppliers for both moisture content and water activity targets. Then ask how they test them.

A useful supplier answer may include:

- target moisture range
- target water activity range
- test method or instrument type
- when samples are tested after production
- how packaging is validated
- whether shelf-life testing includes texture checks

The goal is not to chase the lowest possible number. The useful range is the one a supplier has validated for the fruit, format, package, storage conditions, and intended use.

## What this means for crunch

Crunch is a sensory outcome that can be checked separately from the lab results. When freeze-dried fruit loses crunch, moisture pickup or changing water availability may be part of the explanation, but package integrity, storage conditions, and the product's formulation also matter.

This is why a good quality specification should connect the lab result to the eating experience. A buyer should not only ask, "Is it dry?" The better question is, "What moisture and water-activity range have you validated for this package and storage use?"

## Practical buyer checklist

Connect these measurements to [moisture exposure during handling](/articles/moisture-control/) and to the [pouch's barrier and seal design](/articles/barrier-films-for-freeze-dried-fruit/). A lab value taken on one sample does not replace checking the finished package over storage.

Before approving a freeze-dried fruit supplier, ask:

- What is the finished moisture target?
- What is the finished water activity target?
- Are the numbers measured on every batch or by sampling plan?
- Is the product tested before or after final packaging, and at what sample temperature?
- What packaging barrier is used for humid environments?
- Is desiccant used, and if so, why?
- What texture changes are expected after opening?

The strongest suppliers will not treat these questions as unusual. They will already have a way to explain them.

## Bottom line

Moisture content tells you how much water remains in freeze-dried fruit. Water activity tells you how available that water is under the measurement conditions. Both measurements matter, but neither replaces product-specific validation of packaging, storage, safety controls, or sensory quality.

If a product is being sold as premium, shelf-stable, and consistently crisp, the supplier should be able to explain both specifications, their methods, and the evidence behind the package-and-storage claim.
