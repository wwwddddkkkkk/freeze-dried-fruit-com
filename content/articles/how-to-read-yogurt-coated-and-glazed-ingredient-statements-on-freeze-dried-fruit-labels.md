"Yogurt-coated" and "chocolate-glazed" are among the most appealing phrases on a freeze-dried fruit shelf, and among the most misleading if you read only the front of the pack. The words suggest fruit with a light, wholesome finish. Often the reality is closer to a small confection with some fruit inside. The ingredient statement settles which one you are holding — but only if you read it the way it is actually organized, in descending order of weight and with attention to what a "coating" is really made of.

## The direct answer

Ingredients are listed by weight from most to least, so the fastest read of a coated freeze-dried fruit is simply where the fruit falls in the list. If sugar or a coating base comes before the fruit, the product is mostly coating and sweetener with fruit as a minority. If the fruit is listed first, the balance leans toward fruit. And a "yogurt coating" is almost never just yogurt — it is typically a sugar-and-oil confection carrying a little yogurt powder, often with its own sub-list in parentheses. Read that sub-list, check the added sugars line, and compare coated products against other coated products rather than against plain freeze-dried fruit.

## Order is the whole story

The single most useful rule for a coated or glazed product is that the ingredient list is ranked by weight. The first ingredient is the biggest component; the last is the smallest. On a plain freeze-dried fruit, the fruit is usually first and often the only thing. On a coated product, the picture changes fast. When "sugar," "yogurt coating," or a chocolate base appears ahead of the fruit, the coating outweighs the fruit — you are buying a confection built around fruit, not fruit with a thin finish. When the fruit still leads and the coating ingredients trail behind, more of the weight is genuine fruit.

The front-of-pack name does not change with this order. "Yogurt-covered strawberries" can describe a piece that is mostly strawberry with a thin dusting, or a piece that is mostly sweet coating around a small strawberry center. The ingredient order tells you which, and the marketing name does not.

## What "yogurt coating" actually is

The word "yogurt" carries a health halo that the coating usually does not earn. A typical yogurt coating is a confectionery coating: mostly sugar and a fat or oil, with a modest amount of yogurt powder added for flavor and to justify the name. It is a sweet shell that happens to taste faintly of yogurt, not a serving of yogurt in any nutritional sense.

Because the coating is itself a compound ingredient, it often appears with its own parenthetical sub-list — something like *yogurt coating (sugar, palm kernel oil, yogurt powder, soy lecithin)*. Reading that sub-list is the moment the product becomes clear. Sugar commonly leads it; the yogurt powder is often near the end. The same logic applies to chocolate and "candy" style glazes, which are similarly sugar-and-fat forward.

::: note "Look inside the parentheses"
The headline ingredient name — "yogurt coating," "chocolate coating," "confectionery glaze" — can hide what it is built from. The sub-ingredients in parentheses are where the sugar, the oils, and the allergens (milk and soy are common) actually appear. If you are watching added sugar or avoiding an allergen, read the parenthetical list inside the coating, not just the coating's name.
:::

## The sugar and fruit-percentage shift

Coatings and glazes add sugar and fat, which pushes two numbers in the same direction: added sugars go up and the fruit percentage goes down compared with plain freeze-dried fruit of the same variety. Most of a coated product's sweetness and calories live in the shell. That is not a reason to avoid coated fruit — it is a treat and reasonable to enjoy as one — but it does mean you should read it as a treat. Check the added sugars line on the Nutrition Facts panel, and where a fruit percentage or QUID-style figure is declared, use it. A piece that is half coating by weight is a genuinely different purchase from a plain freeze-dried piece of the same fruit.

## Compare like with like

Because a coated product is a confection and plain freeze-dried fruit is essentially just fruit, comparing them directly is unfair to the coated product and unhelpful to you. Head-to-head on price per gram, sugar, or fruit content, the coated piece loses every measure — and that comparison misses what it is for. The useful comparison is coated against coated: one yogurt-covered fruit versus another, judged on how much real fruit each contains, how much added sugar it carries, and what its coating is actually made of. Keep plain-fruit decisions in one column and treat decisions in another.

## Storage is not automatically the same

A coating changes the surface of the piece and the way moisture moves through it, so a coated product does not necessarily keep like bare freeze-dried fruit — which is extremely moisture-sensitive on its own. Some coatings offer a little surface protection; others add fats with their own storage considerations. The safe approach for a shopper is not to assume, but to follow the storage and best-by guidance printed on the specific pack, and to keep coated pieces sealed and dry as you would any freeze-dried product. Read the label's own instructions rather than applying a plain-fruit rule of thumb, and you will treat the coated product for what the ingredient statement shows it to be.
