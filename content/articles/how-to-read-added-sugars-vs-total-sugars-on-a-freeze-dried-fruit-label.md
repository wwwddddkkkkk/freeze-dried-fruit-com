Turn a bag of freeze-dried strawberries over and the sugar number on the Nutrition Facts panel can stop you short. It often looks high — higher, gram for gram, than the fresh fruit you started with. The instinct is to assume someone dumped sugar in. Usually no one did. The panel has two sugar lines that answer two different questions, and reading them together is the difference between panicking at a plain-fruit product and correctly spotting one that has actually been sweetened.

## The direct answer

Total Sugars counts all the sugar in a serving — the fruit's natural sugar and any added sugar, lumped together. The indented line beneath it, "Includes Xg Added Sugars," breaks out only the sugar that was added during processing. Plain freeze-dried fruit commonly shows a high Total Sugars figure and 0g Added Sugars, because freeze-drying removes water and leaves the fruit's own sugars behind, concentrated into a small serving. So the number to check for "did they sweeten this?" is Added Sugars, not Total Sugars — and you confirm it against the ingredient list.

## Why the total looks so high

Freeze-drying takes water out and leaves everything else in place. The sugar that was dissolved in a fresh strawberry does not go anywhere; it just ends up packed into a much smaller, lighter piece. A serving of freeze-dried fruit represents far more fresh fruit than it looks like, so its natural sugar — reported in Total Sugars — reads high compared with the same weight of fresh fruit.

That is not a red flag by itself. It is the arithmetic of removing water from something that was naturally sweet. The Total Sugars line is doing its job: reporting all the sugar present. What it cannot tell you on its own is where that sugar came from. For that, you drop to the next line.

## What the Added Sugars line is telling you

Under current US labeling rules, the panel must break out added sugars as an indented sub-line reading "Includes Xg Added Sugars," sitting directly under Total Sugars. This line counts only sugar introduced during processing — table sugar, cane sugar, cane juice, honey, syrups, and juice concentrates used to sweeten. Sugars that occur naturally in the fruit are deliberately excluded from it.

For genuinely plain freeze-dried fruit, this line reads 0g. That single figure is the clearest signal on the whole panel that the sweetness is the fruit's own. When it reads anything above zero, the maker put sugar in, and you are looking at a sweetened product — a fruit crisp, a candied piece, or a blend — even if the front of the bag leans on words like "made with real fruit."

::: note "Added Sugars carries a percent Daily Value; Total Sugars does not"
You will see a percent Daily Value next to Added Sugars, based on a reference of 50 grams per day, but no Daily Value beside Total Sugars. That asymmetry is a clue to the label's intent: dietary guidance sets a budget for added sugar specifically, so the panel is built to flag it. Natural fruit sugar shows up in Total Sugars but is not scored against a daily target.
:::

## Reading the two lines together

The two sugar lines make sense only as a pair. Total Sugars tells you how much sugar you are actually eating in a serving — the number that matters if you are tracking total intake. Added Sugars tells you how much of that was the manufacturer's choice rather than the fruit's nature. A product can be high on the first line and zero on the second, and that combination is exactly what pure, unsweetened freeze-dried fruit looks like.

This is why "0g Added Sugars" should not be read as "low sugar." It is a statement about processing, not about quantity. If your reason for checking is total sugar consumption, keep your eyes on Total Sugars. If your reason is avoiding sweetened products, the Added Sugars line is your answer. They are complementary, and confusing one for the other leads people to either fear plain fruit or overlook a sweetened one.

## Confirm it against the ingredient list

The panel and the ingredient list should agree, and checking both closes the loop. Unsweetened freeze-dried fruit lists only the fruit, sometimes with a small amount of an anti-browning aid such as ascorbic acid or citric acid, and nothing else. Its Added Sugars line reads 0g. When those two signals line up, you can trust that the sweetness is natural.

If the Added Sugars line is above zero, expect the ingredient list to name the source — sugar, cane sugar, cane juice, syrup, or a juice concentrate used as a sweetener. Occasionally a front-of-pack claim will imply purity while the ingredients tell a fuller story, so the ingredient list is the tiebreaker. When the two sugar lines and the ingredient list all agree, you know precisely what you are buying: fruit whose sweetness is its own, or fruit that has been sweetened on purpose. For anyone managing sugar intake for a health reason, the honest move is to read Total Sugars for the amount, use Added Sugars and the ingredients to judge the type, and treat medical questions as questions for a qualified professional rather than for the front of the bag.
